# Site design — where things go, and why

The craft rather than the code. None of this is a legal requirement; all of it
is what separates a plan that merely fits from a plan someone enjoys walking
through.

Sources are named per section. Where a number appears it describes a
**relation between things** — a distance, an angle, a width. There are no
object dimensions here on purpose: how big a stage is comes from the
catalogue, never from this file.

## The main stage anchors everything

Put it at the far end of the site, facing the entrance. People then cross the
whole site to reach it and spread out on the way, instead of piling up at the
gate.

The viewing ground in front of it is a **fan, not a corridor**. A narrow deep
strip puts most of the audience too far away and concentrates pressure at the
front; a wide shallow fan gives more people a real view.

Size it from the headcount, not from the stage. Ticket Fairy's worked example:
for 10 000 people at a main stage, the viewing area must be *vastly* larger
than 2 000 m². Cross-check against the density table in `event-standards.md` —
0.46 m² per person is the crush-adjacent floor, so 10 000 people want on the
order of 10 000 m², not 4 600.

## A second stage is a sound problem before it is a space problem

Large festivals put major stages **hundreds of metres apart**, at opposite
ends, each projecting outward.

A documented failure: two medium stages **150 m apart, angled slightly toward
each other**, produced serious bleed. The fix applied was **rotating one stage
about 45°** away.

So:
- Never point two stages at each other.
- If they must be close, set their speaker arrays **perpendicular or
  back-to-back**.
- Aim each stage at its own audience and beyond that at open ground, or at a
  sound sink — a treeline, water.
- A treeline absorbs **high frequencies only**. Bass carries straight through
  trees and over hills, and bass is what travels. Do not treat a hedge as
  acoustic separation.
- A stage at the foot of a gentle slope, firing up the slope, uses the terrain
  as seating and as containment at once.

## Circulation: two ways in, no way to get stuck

- Main thoroughfares carry heavy two-way traffic — **several metres wide**,
  sized for thousands of people per minute at peak.
- Aisles between stalls: **3 to 4.5 m**, so a queue at one stall still leaves
  room to walk past.
- **Every major zone needs at least two routes to it.** One route is a
  chokepoint waiting for its moment.
- **No dead ends.** A crowd that has to turn around accumulates against the
  people still arriving, and that is how a crush starts.
- Keep queues away from entrances, and keep entrances clear of everything
  else. Separate pedestrian and vehicle traffic entirely (FEMA IS-15 p. 2-12).
- Ticket sales and collection go **in line with but separate from** the
  entrances (IS-15 p. 2-12).

## Food and drink: decentralise, always

- **Several small food courts, one near each stage** — never a single large
  one. A single court makes everyone cross the site at the same moment, which
  is both a queue and a crowd-flow problem.
- Cluster stalls **around seating**. People stay longer and spend more, and
  the seating absorbs the queue.
- Angle stalls so a queue **snakes to the side**, not straight back into the
  walkway. Use a dedicated serpentine queue lane where volume justifies it.
- Shade the queue. A line in full sun empties itself.
- Toilets go **away from food service** (IS-15 p. 2-28) — but not so far that
  people will not use them. This is the tension the layout has to resolve.

## Rest zones are where a site becomes pleasant

- Place them **off the main axes**, not on them. A rest area on a
  thoroughfare is neither restful nor passable.
- Circular or horseshoe plans work: they turn their backs on the noise and
  face inward.
- Widely spaced shaded seating, hammocks, low furniture.
- On a hot arid site, shade is not an amenity — it is what makes the ground
  usable at all. Treat shade as infrastructure and place it before the
  decoration.
